\chapter{Design and validation}

The design of a single-stage transmitter is built around a Pierce oscillator (shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pierce_main}); this is a circuit built out of a crystal, two capacitances, and an inverter. It works by inducing a $180^\circ$ phase shift through the crystal and capacitance network and then inverting and amplifying that signal. This circuit has a total $360^\circ$ phase shift and an amplification greater than 1, fulfilling the Barkhausen criterion. The single-stage transmitter makes use of a crystal, and the two capacitances come from the base-emitter capacitance of the transistor and the capacitively tuned LC tank network. The transistor acts as an inverter/amplifier. The base resistor regulates the bias current, and the base RC network makes the oscillator pulse. A detailed explanation of the oscillator circuit can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:crystal}.

	\caption{Schematic of the single-stage Pierce oscillator circuit (taken from \cite{naef-daenzerNewTransmitterSmall1993a}).}
	\label{fig:pierce_main}
\end{figure}

The circuit has multiple requirements to be able to oscillate. The collector side branch needs to be capacitively tuned for the oscillation frequency of the crystal. This means that the LC tank frequency must be lower than the oscillation frequency, which is capped by an upper boundary formed by $L_1$ and the antenna. This relationship is defined in Equation~\ref{eq:osc_bounds}, where $L'_1$ is the equivalent inductance resulting from $L_1$ and the antenna. This is detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:crystal}:\begin{equation}
	\omega_{\text{tank}} < \omega_{\text{osc}} < \omega_{\text{tank}} \sqrt{1 + \frac{L_2}{L'_1}}
	\label{eq:osc_bounds}
\end{equation}In an ideal case, $L_1$ will be tuned to form a resonant circuit with the antenna at the oscillation frequency, but this is not always possible.

The second requirement states that the base-emitter of the transistor needs to be capacitive in the pF order of magnitude. Some base-emitter capacitances of \glspl{bjt} are too low, especially from RF \glspl{bjt}, requiring a capacitor to be placed between the pins.

The transconductance $g_m$ is fundamentally dependent on the DC collector current. By substituting $I_c = \beta I_b$, the transconductance can be expressed directly as a function of the base bias current:

\begin{equation}
	g_m = \frac{I_c}{V_T} = \frac{\beta I_b}{V_T}
\end{equation}

The transistor's transconductance generates an active negative resistance that amplifies the signal to overcome the parasitic losses within the oscillation circuit, primarily the crystal's motional resistance ($R_s$), and the losses caused by the capacitors. The minimum critical transconductance required for oscillation startup is expressed as:

\begin{equation}
	g_{m,\text{crit}} = 4 \omega^2 R_s C_T^2
\end{equation}

A detailed derivation and full parameter analysis are given in Appendix~\ref{app:crystal}.
 
\section{Component selection and calculation}

 This section shows how the calculations and component selection are done in the thesis. First, the crystal and transistor need to be selected. The crystal will determine the operation frequency, which is the third harmonic in this thesis. It is important to choose a crystal with low load capacitance and motional resistance to keep $g_{m,\text{crit}}$ as low as possible. The most important parameter of the transistor is the $f_T$ frequency, which should be 5x to 10x above the operating frequency. Keeping the $C_{cb}$ capacitance as low as possible will also keep $g_{m,\text{crit}}$ to a minimum. All other components are passive and have fewer requirements; only the capacitors and inductors on the collector side still have to function as capacitors and inductors at the operating frequency.

 \subsection{Available parameters}
 
 The following list shows the selected components and the variables given by their specifications:
 
 \begin{itemize}
 	\item \textbf{Crystal:} Gives the oscillation frequency ($f_{\text{osc}}$), static capacitance ($C_0$), and motional resistance ($R_s$).
 	\item \textbf{Transistor:} Gives the feedback capacitance ($C_\mu$) and base-emitter capacitance ($C_\pi$).
 \end{itemize}
 
 Additionally, for the circuit calculations, we assume that the total base capacitance ($C_b$) and the total collector capacitance ($C_c$) are approximately the same ($C_b \approx C_c$). 
 
 With these variables, the external load capacitance ($C_L$) and the total effective parallel capacitance ($C_T$) can be calculated using the following formulas:
 
 \begin{equation}
 	C_L = \frac{C_b C_c}{C_b + C_c}
 \end{equation}
 
 \begin{equation}
 	C_T = C_0 + C_L + C_\mu
 \end{equation}
 
 
 
\subsection{Transconductance Calculation}

The critical transconductance ($g_{m,\text{crit}}$) required for oscillation can be calculated using the parameters established in the previous section (where $\omega = 2\pi f_{\text{osc}}$):

\begin{equation}
	g_{m,\text{crit}} = \omega^2 C_b C_c R_s \left(\frac{C_T}{C_L}\right)^2
\end{equation}

To ensure a reliable oscillator startup under various conditions, the target transconductance ($g_m$) is designed to be five times higher than this critical threshold:

\begin{equation}
	g_m = 5 \times g_{m,\text{crit}}
\end{equation}

Using this target transconductance, the required DC base bias current ($I_b$) and the values for the bias network resistors ($R_1$ and $R_2$) can be determined. The base current is derived using the thermal voltage ($V_T$) and the transistor's common-emitter current gain ($\beta$):

\begin{equation}
	I_b = \frac{g_m V_T}{\beta}
\end{equation}

Assuming a standard series biasing configuration from the supply voltage ($V_{CC}$), the total required bias resistance is determined by Ohm's law. Consequently, the sum of the bias resistors $R_1$ and $R_2$ must equal:

\begin{equation}
	R_1 + R_2 = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{BE}}{I_b}
\end{equation}
